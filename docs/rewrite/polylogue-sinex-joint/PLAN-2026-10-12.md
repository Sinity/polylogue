# How to proceed: a reflection on the Spec work, and a plan for both sessions

Written 2026-10-12 by a fork of the Polylogue session, at the operator's
request, after two days of work on the Spec by two cooperating sessions. It
is meant for the operator and for both agents. The facts below come from
the repository at the time of writing: 767 commits, 34 flake checks, and the
files named.

## 1. What the work produced

- **A very large specification.** Polylogue's `spec/` is about 117,000
  words and its `plan/` 44,000; Sinex's are 129,000 and 50,000. Evidence
  adds about 700,000 more, and the two LOG files 30,000. No person reads
  this end to end, and no agent holds it in one context.
- **Executable checks.** 34 flake checks: example worlds recomputed (Morrow,
  Kestrel), reference query evaluators with composition laws, prototypes in
  Rust and Python, the joint contract test, and many consistency checks of
  the text itself (laws index, outline placement, vision catalogs, type
  drift, today-inventories, builder reproduction).
- **Layers the operator asked for**, built on the last day: vision
  catalogs (Polylogue 123 uses, Sinex 69, joint 13), outlines (Polylogue 60
  components, Sinex 35), compiling skeletons, a joint outline of 17
  interfaces, a joint glossary and joint query laws, and 31 joint decisions.
- **Prototypes on the operator's real data** that changed the design:
  Polylogue's convergence loop over 25 GB of Claude Code sessions, 1,439 real
  Codex rewrites stored as deltas (every rewrite had dropped records), three
  vision prototypes over 1.9 million tool calls, and parsers checked against
  every local provider. Sinex measured its sources, paste attribution,
  credentials and clipboard capture the same way.

## 2. What worked

1. **Running things on real data.** Almost every finding that mattered came
   from running something, not from reading: the collector missing 480
   workflow subagent files, Codex rewrites losing 18,533 tool calls, the
   quadratic rebuild, unpriced Codex usage hiding most of the real spend,
   pasted context counted as typed prose, 0.08% of tool results holding
   credentials. Reading the spec found contradictions; running code found
   the world.
2. **Cross-session review.** The other session's questions were the
   highest-yield review in the whole process. Examples: the frontier
   counter that a rebuild would reset, the command-word hazard, the
   coverage of clipboard capture, partial periods, the browser being
   model-reachable. Each was found by one session looking at the other's
   text from a different angle.
3. **Adopting findings across the boundary.** A defect found in one project
   was checked in the other within minutes, and often found there too
   (command words, partial periods, unparseable times, time precision).
4. **Executable statements of rules.** Where a rule became a check (the
   joint signal evaluator, the masking cases, the frontier, the route move),
   later edits could not quietly break it.

## 3. What was waste, or low value

1. **Reactive ping-pong.** A large share of the last day was one-line rule
   edits in reply to the other session's latest finding, each with its own
   message, commit and acknowledgement. Many were correct, but small, and
   the cadence meant neither session stepped back. The cost was attention:
   dozens of exchanges per hour, each restating context.
2. **Checks of the spec rather than of behavior.** Several flake checks
   verify that the text agrees with itself: law counts, citation targets,
   placement in the outline, quoted totals. They are cheap and they did
   catch real slips (eight unindexed laws, 73 loosely named commands). But
   they are apparatus, and the operator's own history warns about this: the
   reindex campaign spent months building proof machinery around a product
   that did not move. The Spec now has the same tendency in miniature.
3. **Process slips.** Two forks ran at the same time, twice, against the
   operator's explicit rule. One fork committed the index without a
   pathspec and swept in the other session's staged file. One commit broke
   `flake.nix` parsing. One DDL comment edit was not re-copied to the
   prototype. Each was caught, but by luck or by the other session, not by
   routine.
4. **Volume.** About 340,000 words of spec and plan is too much to be the
   thing people and agents read. The size grew by accretion: every finding
   added a paragraph, and almost nothing was removed.

## 4. Where the work drifted from what the operator asked

The operator's requests, collected: an exhaustive but well-structured spec;
top-down, breadth-first layers, progressively more solid; the full vision of
an exocortex substrate and what grows on it, with rigor; value taken from
the old codebases; crisp naming; both projects equally.

- **Top-down came last.** The work went bottom-up for most of its length
  (sources, storage, laws), and the vision, outline and skeleton layers were
  added only on the second day. They exist and are checked, but the lower
  layers were not re-derived from them; they were placed into them.
- **The vision is wide but thin.** Polylogue has 123 uses, but 67 are level
  0 (a catalog line) and only 3 have run on real data. There is no short
  narrative that says, with evidence, why this substrate is worth building
  and what the operator gets in the first month. The operator asked for
  exactly that ("why they are a good idea, if they are").
- **"Exhaustive" was pursued as coverage, not structure.** The
  today-inventories and bead audits give every old surface and task a
  disposition, which is real value. But exhaustiveness made the spec larger,
  not easier to navigate; the structure that would make it readable (a short
  path through it, and provisional far slices) is missing.
- **Design was barely used.** The operator offered Claude Design. The canvas
  boards exist on both sides, but the operator has not checked them, and
  only Sinex's Writing board came out of this round.

## 5. The spec now

- **Coherent enough to build slice 1.** Polylogue's slice 1 (Claude Code end
  to end) has a 401-line slice document, a prototype that already covers
  about half of it (parsers, convergence, store, daemon basics, roughly
  10,000 lines of Rust with 96 tests), a compiling skeleton, and laws
  attached to components. A builder could start tomorrow. The open risks
  are known: the briefing's real usefulness (a trial is running), incremental
  carry for every provider, and batched durability.
- **Over-specified far ahead.** Slices 5–12 (workbench, signals, pages,
  analysis passes, backed mode details) are specified down to field names
  and laws years before code exists. Every such paragraph is a liability:
  it will be wrong in detail by the time it is built, and it attracts more
  edits now.
- **Under-validated where it matters most.** The things that decide whether
  the operator will use the system daily have the least real-data evidence:
  briefings, the web reader, the query language beyond examples, and
  Polylogue-on-Sinex beyond the contract test.
- **Contradictions keep appearing.** Each review found some. That means more
  remain, and that text volume, not care, is the cause.

## 6. How to proceed

The central change: **stop refining the Spec as a document, and start
building against it.** The Spec has passed the point where more text
improves the outcome; code and real data now find more per hour than review
does. From here, the spec for a slice is revised when that slice is built,
from what building it teaches, and far slices stay provisional.

### When the Spec is "done enough"

It is done enough for a slice when: the slice document has no open
blockers; its laws are placed and its prototype parts pass on real data; and
both sessions agree the boundary decisions it touches are stable. Polylogue
slice 1 meets this after the briefing trial and one readiness pass. Sinex
should apply the same test to its slice 1.

### Stop

- Replying to each peer finding with a separate edit and message. Batch.
- Adding new checks that only verify the text agrees with itself, unless a
  real slip has shown the need.
- Refining slices beyond the next two. Mark them provisional instead.
- Running more than one fork at a time.

### Start

- Building slice 1 in the real repositories (Polylogue: the long-lived
  rewrite branch, `decisions/repository.md`), seeded from the prototypes.
- A short reading path per project: one page that names the dozen files a
  builder must read, verified by a fresh agent who builds from it.
- A vision synthesis per project: two or three pages, with the real-data
  prototypes as evidence, saying what the first month of use gives the
  operator and which uses justify slices 1–4.
- A routine after every fork or batch: the full flake check, and commits
  with explicit paths only.

### Polylogue session, in order

1. Finish the briefing trial and apply what it shows. It tests the feature
   agents will meet first.
2. Readiness pass on slice 1: read `plan/slice-01.md` against the prototype
   and skeleton; list and resolve blockers; write a one-page "slice 1 starts
   here" note.
3. Mark slices 5–12 provisional in `plan/slices.md` and their spec sections,
   so they are not refined further until their slice is next.
4. Write the reading path and the vision synthesis (section 6, Start), and
   raise the 6–8 intended uses that justify slices 1–4 to level 2 or 3.
5. With the operator's approval, begin slice 1 in the Polylogue repository.
6. Keep the spec in step with code from then on: a slice's spec changes in
   the same branch as its code.

### Sinex session, in order

1. Apply the same readiness test to Sinex slice 1, and write its one-page
   "starts here" note.
2. Mark Sinex's far slices provisional.
3. Write Sinex's reading path and vision synthesis, using its real-data
   measurements (`vision/real/measurements.py`) as evidence.
4. Keep backed mode at the contract level (the contract test, the joint
   decisions) until Polylogue slices 1–4 exist; refine it then.
5. With the operator's approval, begin Sinex slice 1 in the Sinex
   repository.

### How the two sessions cooperate from here

- **Send a finding only when it changes the other side's spec, contract or
  code.** Send it as a short batch, at most a few times a day, not per item.
- **Joint decisions only for interfaces.** Internal choices stay in each
  project.
- **One owner per joint file or row**, as today; the other reviews.
- **Review at boundaries**, when a slice touching the joint contract is
  ready, rather than continuously.
- Keep the shared prototypes (signals, masking, contract, logical day,
  hashed-blobs) as the place where joint rules are defined by running code.

## 7. Decisions for the operator

1. **Start building.** Approve that Polylogue slice 1, and Sinex's slice 1,
   begin in their real repositories once each passes its readiness pass. If
   you want more spec work first, say what is missing.
2. **Order of work** (Polylogue `questions.md` #6–7): daily use first, then
   the web reader and the workbench, or the workbench sooner. Default: daily
   use first.
3. **Freeze far slices.** Approve marking slices beyond the next two as
   provisional, not refined until they are next.
4. **Old Polylogue history.** The public repository's history still holds
   real session data (`polylogue-s68di`). Rewriting public history is
   yours to decide; neither session will do it unasked.
5. **Design boards.** Look at the canvas boards (both projects), or say they
   can wait until there is a web reader to look at.
6. **Pending names.** Sinex: "occurrence coordinates" → "record address",
   `ts_coided` → `interpreted_at`. Polylogue: whether to rename the
   "reference" link kind (it changes a stored value) and whether the
   workbench keeps the word "page".
